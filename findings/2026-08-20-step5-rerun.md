## Why this note exists

The August 8 finding and the docstring in
`rba-decision-service/src/rba_decision_service/scoring/logreg.py` both quoted
**logreg recall@1%FPR = 0.500** and **freeman = 0.079**. The artifact actually
deployed on the request path — `artifacts/logreg-0.1.0.json`, `trained_at
2026-08-19T05:05:10Z` — carries **0.3947** in its baked operating point, and the
regenerated report agrees. Two different training runs; the thesis was citing the
older one. **Cite the numbers below.**

## What moved, and why

| | Aug 8 | Aug 20 | |
|---|---|---|---|
| logreg ROC-AUC | 0.880 | 0.8725 | noise-level |
| logreg recall@1%FPR | 0.500 (19/38) | 0.3947 (15/38) | **four positives** |
| freeman ROC-AUC | 0.762 | 0.7790 | β retune 10→5 |
| freeman recall@1%FPR | 0.079 (3/38) | 0.1053 (4/38) | **one positive** |

The whole logreg delta is four detections out of 38. At this positive count a
single threshold tick moves recall by 2.6 points, so neither run is "the right
one" in any strong sense — but the deployed artifact is the one whose threshold
is actually in production, so that is the one to quote.

The most likely driver is ADR-0027's **success-only familiarity** in
`rba-features` v0.2.0: a failed login no longer establishes a seen-value or
increments `user_login_count`. That is a security fix — it stops an attacker
normalising their own context by failing repeatedly — and it necessarily changes
every feature vector the baselines are fitted on.

## Recall by history depth

**The two tables count depth differently, by construction.** This is not an
inconsistency in the data; it is two code paths in `ml/train.py`:

- Freeman (`train.py:136`) buckets on `cumcount()` — the user's row index,
  counting **every** login including failures.
- Baselines (`train.py:167`) bucket on the `user_login_count` **feature**, which
  after ADR-0027 counts **successful** logins only.

So the same test set yields 8,750 "first login" rows under one definition and
12,478 under the other. Do not merge these tables.

**freeman** (depth = all prior logins)

| bucket | n | positives | recall |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 (first login) | 8,750 | 8 | 0.0000 |
| 1–2 | 10,343 | 9 | 0.0000 |
| 3–9 | 14,615 | 15 | 0.2667 |
| 10+ | 12,220 | 6 | 0.0000 |

**logreg** (depth = prior *successful* logins)

| bucket | n | positives | recall |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 (first login) | 12,478 | 8 | 0.0000 |
| 1–2 | 10,483 | 9 | 0.7778 |
| 3–9 | 12,641 | 15 | 0.4000 |
| 10+ | 10,326 | 6 | 0.3333 |

With 6–15 positives per bucket these recalls are extremely noisy. The one claim
they support — and it is the thesis-relevant one — is the **logins-to-protection**
story: both models detect **nothing** on a user's first login, because there is
no history to be unusual against. Eight of 38 takeovers are structurally
invisible to behavioural RBA. That is the empirical case for ADR-0027's
supervised second opinion and for treating a cold profile as elevated rather
than as safe.

## Takeaways

- The ranking result holds: an explainable linear model on `*_seen_before`
  features separates takeovers well above chance (ROC-AUC 0.87) at a 0.7%
  challenge rate.
- Freeman remains the label-free primary. Its weakness at the strict operating
  point is exactly why the supervised model escalates rather than replaces
  (ADR-0027).
- Trees still underperform: the mostly-binary features are near-linear in
  log-odds and extreme rarity makes them overfit.
- Report all of this as **feasibility evidence**, not tuned performance. With 38
  positives the confidence intervals are wide enough to swallow most of the
  differences between runs.
